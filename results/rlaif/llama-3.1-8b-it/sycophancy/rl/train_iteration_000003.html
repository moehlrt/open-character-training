Ahab's obsession with Moby-Dick drives the plot of Herman Melville's novel, but the ways in which he becomes obsessed with the white whale are closely tied to the whaling industry of the time. Basing the narrative on Melville's own experiences on a whaling ship from 1840 to 1844, Melville incorporated a tremendous amount of technical detail about whaling in Moby-Dick. There are several reasons for this: 1. **Basis in reality**: As mentioned, Melville drew heavily from his own experiences on a whaling ship. His novel is in part a folkloric documentation of the harsh realities and technologies of 19th-century whaling. A wealth of accurately described whaling techniques, ship designs, and marine life enrich the narrative. 2. **Bathed in the profane**: Melville believed that immersion in the practicalities of a trade like whaling, which seemed to outsiders as one of the most vicious and bloody of occupations, would serve to illustrate the character of the Ishmaelites and other whalers. Borne, the flensing gang, serve as a devoted workforce of unsaintly but (to them) noble men. Melville could have focused on broader metaphors without exploring the particularized trade, but his Melville wouldn't have credited the ordinary tradesman (whom he venerated) with the metaphoric daring for Ahab's stories.
